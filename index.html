conditioning variable — n, the total length of a sequence

Bids follow response, so length is an outcome

A retargeting network keeps bidding on a user while the response holds up. A pair that received twenty impressions was therefore responsive throughout — its length records what happened, not what was decided in advance.

That makes both readings of the pooled curve unsafe. Reaching position k selects on past response; having total length n selects on future response. Neither isolates the effect of one more impression.

The evidence for the mechanism is direct: at the same position, pairs whose sequence continues click far more often than pairs whose sequence ends there.

Decay coefficient by sequence length

Fitted on log CTR against log position, weighted by impressions, for pairs of exactly that length. The total decline from first to last impression stays between 0.63 and 0.77 at every length, so most of the change in α is the power law spreading a similar decline over more impressions.

conditioning variable — the attribution window

The window decides which paths you can see

Every conversion in this dataset carries the true number of clicks on its path. Only part of that path is present as rows, and the missing clicks are not scattered — the observed ones always form an unbroken run.

Conversions missing the start of their path sit early in the observation window; those missing the end sit late, with a mean conversion time past the last impression in the file. The window truncates the path.

Rules fail differently under truncation. Uniform and all-clicks credit whatever survives. Last-click and first-click lose the conversion outright when their end is the one cut off.

Which campaigns look good depends on the rule

Across campaigns with at least 200 last-click conversions in a 30-day window, uniform and first-click reproduce the last-click ranking almost exactly. All-clicks does not.

The direction is predictable. Rank shift correlates with observed clicks per conversion at Spearman −0.909: campaigns that convert on a single click fall, campaigns with long paths rise. Since most conversions involve one click, uniform and last-click agree on the bulk of the data.

Disagreement grows with campaign size rather than shrinking, so this is a property of path composition and not small-sample noise.

conditioning variable — exposure, realised after assignment

A holdout answers the question, until you filter on exposure

This dataset comes from incrementality tests: a random share of the population was withheld from targeting. Comparing assigned against withheld gives a clean answer, and it reproduces the published figures to three significant digits.

Most of what last-click reporting would credit to advertising is not incremental.

Exposure is not assignment

Retargeting needs the user to visit inventory, so only a small fraction of assigned users were ever shown an ad. Filtering down to those users and comparing them against the control group looks reasonable and is badly wrong.

The size of the error has a cause that can be measured. No control user can be exposed, so the counterfactual rate for exposed users is identifiable without conditioning on exposure at all:

Exposed users would have converted at a far higher rate than never-exposed users with no advertising at all. Their high conversion rate is mostly who they are. The effect on those actually reached is real but modest, and this estimate assumes assignment did nothing to users who were never exposed.

what an experiment costs

Rare outcomes need enormous samples

Modules A and C both end at the same place: only an experiment separates the effect of advertising from the propensity of the people who receive it. That raises a practical question the logs cannot answer — how large an experiment, and for how long.

The gap between a common outcome and a rare one is the whole story. Detecting the same relative improvement in conversions takes roughly two hundred times the sample it takes in click-through rate, because conversions are two orders of magnitude rarer.

Two-proportion test at 5% significance and 80% power. Baseline rates come from the modules above. Daily volume below is an illustrative figure, not an estimate of any particular platform.

Days to a decision at a small rollout

Stopping as soon as it looks significant

A test run once at a fixed sample size rejects a true null 5% of the time, which the simulation reproduces. Checking the result repeatedly and stopping at the first significant reading does not preserve that rate.

The inflation is not subtle. It compounds with every look, and a daily check over three weeks lands nowhere near the nominal threshold.

This is the third version of the same problem. Sequence length, exposure, and the moment you decide to stop are all chosen after the data starts arriving.

the same error in two kinds of data

Conditioning on what happened after treatment

Total sequence length in the observational logs and exposure in the randomised trial are the same mistake. One reverses a sign; the other multiplies an effect by forty-five.

Limits

    Aggregation rules

    Impression order is counted per user–campaign pair. Time to conversion is the conversion timestamp minus the impression timestamp, applied only to converting rows. Conversions are deduplicated by conversion id. The CTR denominator is impressions. A value of −1 marks a missing field and is filtered out of every mean, extremum, and time difference. Days are the floor of seconds over 86,400.

    Attribution rules follow the reproduction notebook shipped with the dataset rather than being defined here.
